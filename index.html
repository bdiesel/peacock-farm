About Peacock Farm & Lexington's Mid-Century Modern Heritage

History, architecture, and community — the story behind the houses

The Farm That Became a Neighborhood

In 1951, two young MIT-trained architects — Danforth Compton and Walter Pierce — purchased 42 acres of former dairy farmland on the edge of Lexington, Massachusetts. The land had been used as a dairy farm since the 1800s, and in the early twentieth century its owners kept peacocks on the property — giving the eventual neighborhood its name.

The original c. 1830 farmhouse and barn still stand at the entrance to the neighborhood today. They are the only pre-MCM structures in the historic district.

Compton and Pierce broke ground on a demonstration model at 4 Peacock Farm Road in late 1951. By 1958 they had built more than 50 houses on the site. The development was, by the time of its completion, described as "the third important planned modernist development to be begun in Lexington" — following Six Moon Hill (1948) and Five Fields (1951).

The Design

Compton and Pierce set out to democratize good architecture — to make contemporary design available at a price ordinary families could afford. Their solution was a single, endlessly adaptable floor plan:

  • Split-level with a mid-level entry
  • Low-pitched asymmetrical gable roof
  • Vertical cedar siding
  • Broad horizontal bands of windows
  • Open floor plan — living, dining, kitchen flowing together
  • 3 bedrooms, 1.5 bathrooms, fireplace
  • Approximately 1,800 sq ft

The design was offered in several sizes and configurations. In 1957 it won the AIA (American Institute of Architects) First Award for residential design — one of the most prestigious honors in American architecture.

The Bauhaus Connection

The Peacock Farm houses did not emerge in isolation. They were part of a broader wave of modernism that washed over Lexington starting in the late 1940s — a direct consequence of Walter Gropius arriving at Harvard's Graduate School of Design in 1937.

Gropius, founder of the German Bauhaus school, brought with him a generation of European modernist ideas: open plans, honest materials, integration of indoors and outdoors, and a rejection of historical ornament. His students fanned out across Greater Boston, and Lexington — with its open land and progressive community — became their proving ground.

In 1945 Gropius co-founded The Architects Collaborative (TAC), which designed Six Moon Hill (1948) and Five Fields (1951) — the two neighborhoods that preceded Peacock Farm. Compton and Pierce, trained at MIT under the same modernist ethos, continued the tradition at Peacock Farm.

The Six Neighborhoods

Compton and Pierce's design proved so popular that it spread across Lexington over two decades. The same split-level plan — with minor variations — was built in five additional neighborhoods beyond the original Peacock Farm Historic District:

Lextopia: Lexington's Second Revolution

In 2015 the Lexington Historical Society mounted Lextopia: Lexington's Launch of Mid-Century Modern — a major exhibition celebrating Lexington's extraordinary concentration of modernist architecture. The organizers called it "Lexington's second revolution": if the first was in 1775, the second was the quiet revolution in American domestic life that unfolded here between 1948 and 1979.

May 31, 2015Opening house tour — four private homes designed by Henry B. Hoover
Jun 20 – Sep 20, 2015Main gallery exhibition at The Lexington Depot, 13 Depot Square
Oct 4, 2015Final house tour and marketplace of MCM furniture, housewares, and design objects

Curator Elaine Doran (Lexington Historical Society) and co-curator Rick Beyer assembled artifacts typical of MCM homes — cocktail glasses, ceramic dishes, coffee tables and chairs — alongside archival photographs and architectural drawings. The exhibition captured how Peacock Farm residents "didn't just buy a house — you bought a way of life": a community built around shared stewardship, neighborly connections, and a conscious embrace of modern living.

A follow-on program, At Home with Mid-Century Modern, was presented by the Lexington Historical Society in May 2021.

National Register of Historic Places

The original Peacock Farm neighborhood (Area S) was listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2012, recognizing its significance as a cohesive planned modernist community. The nomination was prepared by the Massachusetts Historical Commission (MHC).

The district encompasses 63 contributing buildings: 48 standard Compton & Pierce split-levels, 7 A-Series single-story variants, 4 custom designs, 1 Techbuilt, and the original 1830 farmhouse and barn.

MHC survey numbers: LEX.S and LEX. 548–550, 2033–2095. The remaining five neighborhoods (Areas AK–BA) have been surveyed by the Lexington Historical Commission but are not part of the National Register district.

Lexington's Broader MCM Legacy

Peacock Farm was part of a remarkable cluster of mid-century modern neighborhoods that made Lexington one of the densest concentrations of post-war modernist housing in America:

NeighborhoodYearArchitects
Six Moon Hill1948The Architects Collaborative (TAC) / Walter Gropius
Five Fields1951TAC — utopian community experiment
Peacock Farm1952Compton & Pierce (MIT)
Middle Ridge1955Carl Koch
Shaker Glen / Rumford / Grove / Pleasant Brook1957–79Compton & Pierce (MIT)

More than 200 mid-century modern houses survive in Lexington today. The Lexington Historical Commission has been systematically surveying and documenting these neighborhoods since the 2000s.
